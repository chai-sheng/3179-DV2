02 / WHEN

Follow the season,
not a single timetable.

Australia’s seasons run opposite to those in the Northern Hemisphere. The main pulse in this sample falls in winter and spring, but species and places have different windows.

92.5%

of 2016–2025 observations were recorded from June to October.
This describes the sample; it is not a probability of seeing a whale.

03 / EVIDENCE

More reports.
A richer picture, with limits.

The record grows over time, but a growing online community can also produce more observations. This dataset cannot tell us whether tourism or whale populations grew.

An uneven window on the ocean

66.1%

of baseline records come from 2023–2025.

The data are useful for exploring recorded encounters. A measured tourism trend would need visitor, vessel-trip or spending data from a separate source.

04 / PROTECT

The journey needs
more than open water.

Whales need places to travel, feed, rest and reproduce. Biologically Important Areas identify these uses—and help explain why responsible viewing matters.

Space to do the essential things

20 migration.
15 foraging.
5 resting.
4 reproduction.

The selected records describe different habitat needs. Watching whales responsibly means allowing those behaviours to continue.

Take the story to the coast

Watch with curiosity.
Leave room for the whale.

Use the season as a starting point, check local conditions, and follow current whale-watching guidance. Avoid disturbing feeding, resting or breeding animals. A memorable encounter should leave their journey uninterrupted.
